Greenhouse Effect & CO₂ Myth Testing

A step-by-step investigation testing popular tabletop CO₂ greenhouse demonstrations using sealed PET bottles, IR sources, and thermal probes.

Phase 1: The Question & Initial Setup

It all started after coming across dozens of popular science videos on YouTube showing a straightforward demonstration: place two plastic bottles side by side—one filled with ordinary air and one with high concentrations of carbon dioxide—shine a light on them, and watch the CO₂ bottle get significantly hotter. It looked like an elegant way to show the greenhouse effect in action, but I wanted to test it myself to see if it really worked that easily.

Phase 2: First Trial & The Unexpected Result

I set up two identical transparent PET bottles and fitted both with digital thermometer probes to log temperature changes accurately. I left one bottle filled with room air and filled the second bottle with over 80% CO₂. Setting up a standard visible light source, I turned on the lamp and monitored the readings. To my surprise, both bottles heated up at almost the exact same rate. There was no noticeable temperature gap whatsoever between the air bottle and the high-CO₂ bottle.

Phase 3: Troubleshooting & Shifting to Infrared

Assuming I made a mistake, I went back to the physics fundamentals and checked the absorption spectrum of CO₂. CO₂ doesn't absorb visible light efficiently—it absorbs specific thermal infrared wavelengths. Realizing standard visible lamps might not be giving the CO₂ anything to trap, I adjusted the setup by adding an IR radiation source alongside the visible light. I ran the test again, expecting to finally see the CO₂ bottle climb higher in temperature. Once again, the readings between the two bottles remained completely identical.

Phase 4: Connecting the Dots & Final Conclusion

This led to a clear realization: both bottles were simply trapping heat because they were sealed plastic containers. The primary heating mechanism in this setup was the plastic walls blocking air movement (convection)—which is how actual garden greenhouses work, but not how atmospheric greenhouse gases operate. In the real atmosphere, water vapor plays by far the biggest role in the natural greenhouse effect, and a small 1-liter PET bottle simply doesn't provide enough path length for a gas like CO₂ to produce a measurable radiative temperature rise. The popular YouTube experiments were fundamentally flawed, mistaking basic air containment for gas-driven warming.
