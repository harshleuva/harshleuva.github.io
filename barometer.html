Building a Barometer: Flaws & Calibration

A step-by-step journey from a failed DIY balloon barometer to a dual-responding thermoscope—and how we calibrated a chart to isolate true atmospheric pressure changes.

Materials Required

  • Glass jar / bottle
  • Rubber balloon or flexible membrane
  • Rubber band & tape
  • Drinking straw (used as a pointer)
  • Paper ruler or scale card
  • Clear tube & colored liquid (for Thermoscope)

The Experiment Story & The Science

1. The First Attempt: Glass Jar + Balloon Barometer

We started with the classic textbook setup: stretch a rubber balloon tightly over a glass jar, tape a straw horizontally on top as a pointer, and place a vertical scale behind it. The idea was simple—when weather pressure goes up, it pushes down on the balloon and lifts the straw pointer. When pressure drops, air inside pushes up and the straw points down.

2. Why It Failed: The Temperature Problem

In practice, this simple setup completely fails as a pure barometer. Daily atmospheric pressure changes are very small (often just 30–50 mbar), but room temperature swings easily by 10–30 °C. Because air expands when warm and contracts when cold, temperature changes override the tiny pressure signals. A hot afternoon makes the jar expand inside, pointing up even when pressure didn't change at all!

3. The Upgrade: Building a Thermoscope

Instead of ignoring temperature, we embraced it by building a thermoscope setup (a sealed air container connected to a vertical liquid column). This setup reacts smoothly to both temperature and atmospheric pressure variations together.

4. The Solution: Filtering Temp with a Calibration Chart

To extract true pressure readings, we created a Calibration Chart. By recording room temperature alongside the device reading, we can subtract the predicted temperature effect using standard gas laws ($PV = nRT$). The remaining shift gives an accurate measurement of isolated atmospheric pressure changes!
