What is a Tesla Turbine?

Patented by Nikola Tesla in 1913, the Tesla turbine (also known as the boundary-layer turbine, friction turbine, or cohesion-adhesion turbine) is a unique, bladeless rotary engine. Traditional turbines rely on high-pressure fluid striking angled blades or buckets to force a shaft to turn. In contrast, a Tesla turbine consists of a series of smooth, parallel circular discs mounted closely together on a central shaft. Fluid (gas, steam, or air) enters tangentially at the outer rim of the discs, spirals inward through the narrow gaps, and exhausts through ports located near the center shaft.

How Does It Work?

The turbine operates entirely on two core principles of fluid mechanics: Adhesion and Viscosity.

  • Boundary Layer Adhesion: When a fluid flows past a solid surface, molecular attraction causes a microscopically thin layer of fluid to stick to the metal (or disc) surface. This immobile layer creates a zero-velocity zone right at the disc boundary.
  • Viscous Drag & Inward Spiral: As outer fluid layers pass over this trapped boundary layer, fluid viscosity creates shear stress. The moving fluid transfers its momentum to the discs through surface friction, pulling the rotor around.
  • Logarithmic Spiral Trajectory: As the fluid loses kinetic energy and transfers momentum to the turning discs, it naturally slows down and spirals inward toward the central exhaust ports in a continuous curve.

Why Is It Not Widely Used Today?

While theoretical models project exceptionally high thermal efficiency under ideal conditions, several key engineering hurdles prevent widespread industrial adoption:

  • Material Centrifugal Distortion: To achieve optimal power output, the discs must spin at ultra-high speeds (often tens of thousands of RPMs). At these speeds, centrifugal forces cause metal discs to warp, stretch, or wobble, narrowing or ruining the essential gap clearance between them.
  • Narrow High-Efficiency Window: The boundary layer effect relies on precise fluid spacing (often fractions of a millimeter). Any fluctuation in load, fluid viscosity, or rotational speed drastically drops the overall efficiency.
  • Lower Power Density: Bladed turbines extract massive amounts of torque per unit volume because high-pressure steam strikes the blades directly. A Tesla turbine requires a significantly larger surface area (many discs) to achieve comparable mechanical power output.

Prototype Specifications & Features

  • Bladeless Rotor Stack: Repurposed compact discs (CDs) stacked on a central shaft using boundary layer adhesion for propulsion.
  • Structural Safety Enclosure: Protective housing built to sustain high rotational kinetic energy safely during high-RPM testing.
  • Reinforced Discs: Applied a protective adhesive layer across disc surfaces to prevent shattering under centrifugal forces.
  • Open Airflow Design: Exposed rotor region designed specifically to track fluid dynamics and boundary layer behavior visually.
  • Power Generation Unit: Mechanical output coupled to a small motor to light up LEDs when driven by high-speed airflow.

Process & Flow Visualization

Aerodynamic Flow Visualization: Introduced smoke from incense sticks across exposed discs to observe airflow boundary layer interaction and spiral trajectories. 4. High-Speed Testing & Power Coupling: Applied high-velocity air streams to spin up the rotor, successfully driving a motor to illuminate LEDs.
