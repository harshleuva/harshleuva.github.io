The Science Behind a Simple DIY Rain Gauge

Building a rain gauge from a plastic bottle, scale, and paper—and exploring key scientific questions: Does shape matter? Why straight walls? And why measure in mm/inches instead of liters?

Scientific Insights

1. The DIY Model: Straight Bottle + Scale

We built a straightforward rain gauge by cutting the top off a plastic bottle, turning the top funnel inside out to catch rainwater smoothly, and sticking a standard ruler on the side. But making the tool was only step one—the real learning came from probing why it is constructed this way through a dedicated worksheet.

2. Does the Shape or Size of the Collector Matter?

A key question in our worksheet: "If two containers of different widths are kept out in the same rain, will they catch the same height of water?" The students discovered that a wider top catches more water volume, but it also has a proportionately larger base to fill. Thus, the height (depth) of accumulated water remains exactly the same regardless of the surface area!

3. Why Must the Walls Be Vertically Uniform?

While overall width doesn't change the depth, slanted walls break a standard ruler scale. If the container widens or narrows towards the top (like a cone or spherical glass), equal height increments no longer correspond to equal volume increments. To use a linear ruler directly without complex volume conversion equations, the walls must be straight and vertically uniform.

4. Why Measure Rainfall in mm/Inches and Not Liters?

If we measure rain in liters or gallons, the answer depends entirely on the size of the container or the land area you measure over. Measuring in millimeters or inches measures height per unit area ($\text{Volume} / \text{Area} = \text{Height}$). $1\text{ mm}$ of rainfall means if rain fell on a flat, non-porous ground without running off, it would form a $1\text{ mm}$ deep layer of water—making the unit area-independent and universal worldwide.
